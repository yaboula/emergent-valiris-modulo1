# Executive conclusion  
The evidence indicates that **customer no-shows and fragmented reservation workflows** are the core problems for small restaurants (P-01, P-03).  No-shows waste seats and revenue – one industry analysis reports that 76% of venues lose on average £3.6K each from empty tables.  Because many bookings still come by phone or ad hoc channels (35–60% by call), restaurants often juggle paper, calendars or spreadsheets, leading to double-booking and lost calls (each missed call is a lost table).  Current tools partly address this: large platforms like TheFork capture online demand but impose commissions and retain customer data.  Specialist systems (CoverManager, Restoo, Tableo) offer deposits, reminders and multi-channel syncing, but they target larger operations or require training.  For our local independent-restaurant segment, the **opportunity** is a leaner booking solution that unifies channels, minimizes no-shows (via reminders or deposits), and embeds into daily workflow without heavy overhead.  However, we face uncertainties: some owners may resist deposit policies (7% of diners avoid reservations if a deposit is required), and many small cafés rely on pure walk-in service.  We lack data on how many local independent restaurants would actually pay for or use advanced features (like AI call-taking or dynamic deposits).  Key hypotheses to test include whether *automated reminders or modest deposits can meaningfully reduce no-shows without deterring customers*, and whether an integrated system substantially saves staff time over current manual methods.

# Module boundary  
This research focused strictly on a **restaurant table-booking module** handling reservations (made online or in person/phone by staff), including table zoning, group size, date/time, changes, cancellations, waitlists, and optionally payment deposits or guarantees. We assumed integration with existing schedule (shifts) and floor plan data from the core system, but did not design the core itself. Outside scope are other services such as in-restaurant POS orders, delivery/takeaway, invoicing, or general time-clocks – only reservation workflow and its touchpoints were considered.  Also excluded are unrelated marketing or loyalty features beyond what directly affects bookings.

# Users, situations and current workflows  
**Who:** Users include restaurant owners/managers, floor staff (hosts/maîtres), and diners.  Small *casual restaurants* and *cafés* represent the target: they serve local repeat clientele and have limited tech budgets.  These businesses typically have one main point of sale and kitchen, and may or may not have a host. Customers may be regulars familiar with the place, or new visitors who found the restaurant online or by word-of-mouth. 

**Tasks/Situations:** Key scenarios include (a) **Making a reservation** – by phone, in person (on a future visit), via the restaurant’s website or social media, or through third-party platforms (e.g. TheFork); (b) **Managing the reservation book** – ensuring tables and zones are allocated, tracking number of covers and special requests; (c) **Handling changes** – customer calls/email to modify/cancel a reservation; (d) **Dealing with no-shows/waitlists** – ensuring empty tables are filled if possible (call waitlisted customers, or seat walk-ins); (e) **Collecting deposits/guarantees** – for large parties or special dates, some restaurants want to hold a credit card or charge a small fee. Restaurants aim to avoid overbooking but also maximize occupancy.

**Current workflows:** In many small restaurants, the workflow is *manual or semi-manual*. Common practices include: keeping a **paper diary or whiteboard** of bookings, updating an **Excel sheet or Google Calendar**, or using a very simple “bookings” app.  Phone reservations often dominate: one analysis estimates 35–60% of bookings come by phone.  Hosts often answer calls during service, which can lead to missed calls when busy.  Online bookings (via Google Business, Facebook, or TheFork) may be recorded separately if at all.  There is typically no unified view: a staffer might not know if a caller has already booked online, leading to double-booking. Cancellations are noted by hand or verbally; no systematic confirmation/reminder is sent. Waitlists (if any) are scribbled on paper. Payment (if required) is usually handled at arrival: few take cards or deposits in advance, so a no-show yields lost revenue. Only larger or tech-savvy venues use dedicated software.  Examples of alternatives used today include: basic tools like Google Forms/Sheets, no reservation system at all, or hand-written ledgers. No standardized, well-integrated solution exists in most small establishments.

# Evidence-backed problems  
- **P-01: Lost revenue from no-shows.** *Users:* Restaurant managers. *Situation:* Busy service nights see a few no-shows or late cancellations. *Problem:* Empty seats cannot be refilled on short notice, directly losing income. E.g., an industry analysis reports restaurants lose 5–20% of potential revenue to empty tables, costing ~£3,600 per venue annually. This demoralizes staff and hurts the bottom line. *Counter-evidence:* Some restaurants find that strict cancellation policies or overbooking by a small margin can mitigate losses, but these risk customer dissatisfaction. *Confidence:* High (multiple sources).

- **P-02: Dependence on high-commission platforms / lost customer data.** *Users:* Owners of small independent restaurants. *Situation:* Many have signed up for TheFork or similar to gain visibility. *Problem:* These platforms charge ~€1.50–4 per cover plus monthly fees, even for regulars. Restaurant loses direct relationship to diners (customer data stays with platform). If the restaurant leaves the platform, it “goes with empty hands” – no booking history or contact list. *Counter-evidence:* Platforms provide valuable reach and convenience; for new or touristy restaurants, being on TheFork or Google can be essential acquisition channels. Some restaurants accept the trade-off as marketing expense. *Confidence:* High.

- **P-03: Missed phone bookings / inefficiency.** *Users:* Hosts/waiters during service. *Situation:* A host is busy serving and a reservation call comes in. *Problem:* If they don’t answer (or call goes to voicemail), a potential reservation is lost: “an unpicked call is another table won by the competition”. Industry commentary warns 80% of callers have not booked through any digital channel, so lost calls are real lost revenue. Juggling call-taking and service also distracts staff. *Counter-evidence:* Some restaurants use a dedicated phone line or have multiple hosts, reducing this problem. Also, if walk-in traffic is strong, missed calls matter less. *Confidence:* Medium.

- **P-04: Overbooking / double-booking risks.** *Users:* Managers coordinating multiple channels. *Situation:* A diner booked via Google and then calls to confirm. *Problem:* Without a unified system, the second reservation might be entered as new, causing two bookings for the same table/time. Overlaps can happen especially when manually syncing phone/web entries. *Counter-evidence:* Many operations keep the paper diary as “source of truth”, ignoring new digital requests if already full. But this is error-prone. *Confidence:* Medium (anecdotal).

- **P-05: Rigid table/allocation rules in existing tools.** *Users:* Operations managers. *Situation:* A restaurant offers both 2h à la carte and a fixed 3h tasting-menu service. *Problem:* Many systems use static seating rules and can’t easily handle mixed service durations or multiple zones (indoor/outdoor/privates). This leads to under- or over-utilization. A marketing analysis argues that static policies (fixed deposit amounts, inflexible layouts) cost restaurants sales and satisfaction. *Counter-evidence:* Smaller venues often use simple uniform rules and can handle adjustments manually with little loss. *Confidence:* Medium.

- **P-06: Lack of enforced deposits/guarantees.** *Users:* Managers of restaurants with large-party bookings. *Situation:* A 10-person group books on a weekend without paying in advance and then partly cancels. *Problem:* Without a deposit or card guarantee, the restaurant cannot recoup lost seats. Behavioral research shows ~30% of diners are more likely to honor a booking when a deposit is at risk, yet most small restaurants lack easy deposit collection. *Counter-evidence:* Some owners fear putting guests off; indeed, ~7% of diners say they avoid booking if a deposit is required. Also, administratively handling deposits/card holds may be complex for non-tech venues. *Confidence:* High on the problem (if no deposit, risk is unmitigated); uncertain on how customers will react.

- **P-07: Time burden of manual processes.** *Users:* Restaurant owners and staff. *Situation:* After each service, staff spend hours updating reservation lists, calling no-shows, and preparing tomorrow’s seating plan. *Problem:* Valuable staff time is wasted on routine tracking. A comparison of booking systems suggests advanced automation (AI assistant, optimized seating) could save 8–10 hours per week per venue. *Counter-evidence:* Not all operations have high volume; some may not need heavy optimization. *Confidence:* Medium.

# Existing solutions  
We reviewed both specialized tools and multi-module suites:

- **TheFork (ElTenedor)** – A well-known *reservation marketplace* owned by Tripadvisor (recently sold to Amex). Over 65,000 Spanish restaurants use it. It brings visibility but has layered fees: commission per cover (≈€1.50–4 plus VAT, depending on plan) and a monthly plan (up to ~€75/month). It also encourages discount promotions (lowering average spend while commissions apply to full price). Critically, TheFork holds guest data and email addresses; restaurants pay for returning customers they already know. It does *offer* a rudimentary “Manager” system, but this is tightly coupled with the booking marketplace model. It supports mobile apps for hosts but generally lacks flexible deposit/AI features (deposits are “configurable” per booking, and phone AI is not provided). Its strength is new customer reach; its weakness is cost and lack of CRM ownership.

- **OpenTable** – Global brand focused on medium/upscale markets. In Spain it has niche presence in tourist cities (Madrid, Barcelona). Pricing is opaque but involves per-cover fees and modest monthly rates. It is **commission-based** on diner count, and less used by local diners. It allows some integration (e.g. Google Reserve) but is largely a paid marketplace. It handles basic availability and reminders but lacks advanced scheduling or deposit logic unless supplemented by premium plans. Analysts note its value is mainly for high-end establishments, not typical local cafés.

- **CoverManager** – A Spanish SaaS platform (used by ~16,000 Spanish restaurants). It is a full table-booking system (no marketplace) with **no commission** on direct bookings. Features include: custom floor plans, multi-zone support, client CRM, email/SMS campaigns, and (in higher plans) an AI phone-booking assistant.  CoverManager explicitly supports deposits/credit cards; it advertises up to 80% reduction in no-shows with its deposits+reminders combo. Plans run roughly €49–299/month, making it cost-effective for medium-high volume venues but possibly expensive for tiny cafés. It integrates with Google/Instagram for online booking, and with POS for check-in if needed. It requires some initial setup and training of staff. Its published strength is comprehensive control (CRM, analytics); a downside is that smaller owners may never use all features, paying for unused complexity.

- **Restoo** – A cloud booking system (2,500+ restaurants globally, incl. Spanish hotels, beach clubs) that emphasizes *zero commissions*. It is not a public directory; restaurants embed it on their own website, Google, Instagram, etc. Key features: unified dashboard for all reservations, dynamic waitlist, native support for card guarantees, and a Bookline-powered phone AI service. The reviewer notes Restoo documents “up to 98%” no-show reduction via deposits and follow-ups. Pricing is usage-based (from ~€39–249/month, according to plan complexity). It lacks a consumer-facing app, so it sacrifices discovery for control. It shines for data ownership and loyalty-building, but lacks the marketing boost of a directory.

- **Tableo** – A freemium Spanish booking app. Its **free plan** covers up to 100 covers/month (suitable for very small venues). Features include online booking widget, Google/Instagram integration, multilingual support, and email/SMS reminders. It interfaces with Stripe for credit-card deposits/payments (see Stripe logo). Tableo centralizes phone, in-person and online bookings in one digital calendar. It is mobile-friendly and very easy to adopt; pay plans are required above the 100-covers threshold. It won’t have enterprise bells (no phone AI, basic CRM), but it directly attacks the “paper and pencil” workflow. It is widely used internationally and boasts major hotel clients, suggesting reliability. Limitations: customization is limited, and customer support is self-service heavy.

- **Revo Flow (Cegid)** – Part of the Revo POS suite. It includes a **reservations module** (Revo Slot/Flow) integrated with the cash register. It supports Google Bookings (reservations made on Google go straight into Revo), web-booking forms, and an incoming waitlist feature (coming soon). It handles booking statuses (pending/confirmed/seated) and prints seating charts. A demo description notes that it “centralizes and automates management, optimizes availability in real time, and sends confirmations and reminders”. Pricing is bundled with POS software (no standalone price). Its advantage is seamless integration with orders and check-in; drawback is that small venues would need the whole POS ecosystem and associated costs. Current documentation does not emphasize deposits explicitly, focusing more on queue control.

- **Other tools:** Generic calendars or WhatsApp are often used as crude solutions, but we did not find strong published evidence of their efficacy in hospitality. (For example, some establishments use a shared Google Calendar or spreadsheet – no formal source.) Multi-purpose scheduling apps (Setmore, Calendly) exist, but lack restaurant-specific features (floor plans, group sizing). We did not identify a well-known Spanish competitor beyond the above; many small restaurants remain on paper or basic digital logs.

It advises **automated reminders and online pre-payment** as remedies. Specifically, it notes that requiring clients to *“pay upfront”* greatly increases commitment: “Clients who pay in advance are less likely to cancel without notice”. This parallels restaurants: a tiny deposit or card hold can make the reservation feel real to the customer (raising perceived cost of no-show). The blog exemplifies the benefit of integrated payment/reminder features. *Context:* salon owners and service businesses; date: 4 May 2026; though a vendor blog, it reflects common-sense reasoning.

- **R-02 (Industry blog by D. Gribble, Mar 2026).** An analysis in *Hospitality Marketing Insight* reports that in 2023, **76% of UK venues** suffered no-shows, losing about £3,621 each. It estimates 5–20% of revenue is lost to empty tables per restaurant. The blog confirms that **larger groups are at higher no-show risk** – operators often require deposits for parties of 6+. Under “Do deposits reduce no-shows?”, the author states *yes*: deposits act as a commitment device, turning a booking “into something of value that the guest risks losing.” Importantly, “30% of diners say the risk of losing a deposit makes them more likely to honor a reservation”. *Context:* hospitality marketing consultant; provides industry stats and consumer psychology. Benefit noted: deposit feature is empirically linked to higher attendance.

However, it also warns of backlash: about 7% of patrons avoid booking if a deposit is required. It cites a viral case where a $250 cancellation fee led to negative online reviews. Shockingly, “94% of consumers avoid a business after seeing negative feedback”. This suggests rigid deposit/cancellation rules can harm reputation. *Context:* industry analysis; implies design trade-offs (commitment vs customer goodwill).

This suggests a gap: *real-world testimonials from independent eateries*, especially non-English sources. Relying on these business/marketing sources introduces bias (promotion of certain solutions). The experiences above are fairly consistent in portraying no-shows as a costly problem and deposit/reminder solutions as beneficial.

# Legal and provider constraints  
- **Data protection (EU/Spain):** Collecting reservation info (names, phone, email) is personal data under GDPR (Regulation (EU) 2016/679). Restaurants must have a lawful basis (e.g. contract performance or legitimate interest) and secure consent if sending marketing (e.g. newsletters or SMS offers). Automatic reminders *may be considered transactional* (allowed to fulfill the service) if limited to the booking context, but any promotional messaging requires clear opt-in (as per Spanish LSSI/LOPDGDD rules). All personal data storage must comply with security standards and retention limits. Client data must not be shared without consent; using an external SaaS (like Stripe or WhatsApp) requires joint-controller arrangements or processors agreements. *Provider policy:* If integrating WhatsApp, one must follow Meta’s Business Messaging guidelines (customers must opt-in to receive messages via WhatsApp).

- **Payment & deposits:** If the module collects deposits or cards, payment regulation applies. Under **PSD2 (EU Payment Services Directive 2, 2018)** strong customer authentication is required for online card payments. A deposit is an electronic payment, so SCA (two-factor auth) would normally be required at charge time. If using a payment gateway (e.g. Stripe), the system must not store CVV data and must use tokens. PCI DSS standards obligate secure handling of card data. Charges to guests (for no-shows) must honor consumer protections; customers can dispute unfair charges (e.g. if cancellation was legitimate). The module should clearly display cancellation and refund policy in advance to avoid disputes (as required by Spanish consumer law, RD 1/2007). Spanish law prohibits abusive terms, so deposit forfeiture must be proportional and communicated at booking.

- **Consumer regulations:** Under Spanish Consumer Protection (e.g. Ley General para la Defensa de los Consumidores), any required prepayment or fee must be clearly communicated at booking. Advanced charges might be considered a “contrato de suministro anticipado” – if so, they become part of the sale of future goods (the meal) and thus refundable if service is delivered. Clarification needed whether a no-show deposit is a fee or partial payment; legal counsel would be advisable.

- **Platform terms:** If integrating with Google Reserve or Facebook, the module must comply with their terms (e.g. Google requires no commission fees when accepting reservations via Google). Similarly, if embedding on social media, policies about messaging, booking flows, and privacy apply.

Overall, the main constraints are (a) **data protection** and **communication consent**, (b) **payment security (PSD2/PCI)**, and (c) **consumer-rights on prepayments**. We did not find specific legislation text on deposits for restaurants, which suggests it’s unregulated territory beyond general contract law; still, careful design is needed to avoid legal exposure (e.g. weaken hypothesis H-* if deposit policies violate consumer fairness). *Note:* Our sources lacked any Spanish law quote; these points are derived from general EU/Spanish rules. Before deployment, legal review is recommended.

# Opportunities and falsifiable hypotheses  
- **H-01: Integrated reminders reduce no-shows.** *Basis:* Both industry sources (R-01, R-02) emphasize reminders as an easy win. Hypothesis: Automated email/SMS reminders (and possibly WhatsApp) will measurably lower no-show rates (e.g. by 10–20%). *Assumption:* Guests sometimes simply forget or are less committed without a prompt. *Test:* A/B test an automated reminder feature on random reservations; measure attendance rates. *Counter-evidence:* Some customers may not read reminders (mobile number may be wrong), limiting effect. If no-shows persist despite reminders, it weakens the return on adding this feature.

- **H-02: Offering optional deposits will improve revenue retention without significant drop in bookings.** *Basis:* Behavioral research indicates deposits increase commitment (R-02/R-03: 30% of diners say deposits make them more likely to show). Hypothesis: If high-demand time slots or large groups are asked for a small deposit, net no-shows will fall enough to pay for any drop in bookings. *Assumption:* The “loss aversion” effect outweighs the friction of prepayment. *Test:* Pilot a deposit policy for a subset of reservations and track cancellation vs. booking decline. *Counter-evidence:* Some customers might choose not to book if deposit required (R-03, 7% avoid booking), especially for routine meals. A significant conversion drop would falsify the hypothesis.

- **H-03: Centralizing all channels will increase operational efficiency.** *Basis:* P-03/P-04 show fragmented channels cause lost bookings and time waste. Hypothesis: A unified dashboard for phone, online, walk-ins (as in CoverManager/Tableo) will save staff time and reduce double-bookings. *Assumption:* Staff will actually use one system rather than separate logs. *Test:* Time-motion study before/after deployment in a pilot restaurant. Monitor number of booking conflicts and time spent per reservation. *Counter-evidence:* If staff fail to adopt the new system (due to training issues or resistance), the potential efficiency gains won’t materialize, weakening this.

- **H-04: Smaller restaurants prefer free or low-cost solution, even with fewer features.** *Basis:* Tableo’s free tier is popular, implying price sensitivity. Hypothesis: Many independent restaurant owners will only adopt if the cost is negligible or tied to usage (≪€50/mo). High fixed fees (>€100) will be a barrier. *Assumption:* The segment has low budgets and tech-lagging attitudes. *Test:* Survey or pilot small venues with varying pricing models. *Counter-evidence:* If many cite feature richness (AI phone, CRM) as worth the cost, then a premium option could succeed. If uptake among small pilot restaurants is low despite free/cheap, then price isn't the only barrier.

- **H-05: Walk-in-focused cafés have low interest in reservation module.** *Basis:* The brief distinguishes “cafés (mostly walk-in)” vs. restaurants with reservations. Hypothesis: A significant share of small cafés will not use the module because their customer flow doesn’t justify it. *Assumption:* Busy cafes don’t block tables for future; they want quick turnover. *Test:* Engagement metrics and feedback from an alpha targeting cafes. *Counter-evidence:* If even “walk-in” venues see value (e.g. managing lunch lines or weekend brunch), then the hypothesis is false and the module should cater to them too.

Each hypothesis must be tested with real users or data before finalizing requirements. For example, if H-02 fails (deposit repels customers), any deposit feature must be optional or carefully tiered. If H-01 is weak (reminders have little effect), then investment should pivot to other features. Null results or negative feedback must force us to rethink assumptions.

# Implications for M0.3  
The evidence suggests several product questions for the next design phase:

- **Deposit/Payment design:** We must decide *if and when* to require deposits. Should the module *enable* deposits as an option (configurable per booking or party size)? Or assume free bookings by default and only allow card-on-file for cancellation penalties? What payment gateway/integration will be used (e.g. Stripe)? We need to validate deposit acceptability (H-02) with target customers to set defaults.

- **Channel integrations:** Ensure alignment with the core’s data: need to integrate with Google/Facebook (for “Reserve” links) and possibly a WhatsApp API for bookings or reminders. Also tie into the core’s *customer records*: if a diner exists in CRM, link the booking. We should confirm which external services (Google, WhatsApp, payment) we plan to integrate, and check their terms (e.g. no-fee clauses). If partner APIs exist (like Revo’s Google integration), leverage them; otherwise, this may need manual setup.

- **User interface & workflows:** The module will be used by hosts on tablets/phones and by diners online. We need to define the reservation form fields and staff view: for instance, show a real-time floor plan and table status. The evidence shows a need for features like waitlist management and reminders. We should prototype and test these flows with staff. Confirm also if offline/walk-in flow is needed (staff adding reservations on the fly, scanning QR codes at arrival as CoverManager offers).

- **Data requirements:** The module needs core input: *table/zone definitions*, *opening hours* (for availability windows), *turnover time per booking*, *max capacity per seating*. M0.3 must ensure the core stores this or the module allows configuring it. We should ask: do we need to handle multiple seating shifts (lunch/dinner)? The evidence suggests yes, some restaurants need multi-duration slots.

- **Training & usability:** A recurring theme is that advanced features only help if used. The design must balance power with simplicity. We might plan for an initial onboarding/training module for staff. Also, consider a separate “self-service” booking page for customers that can be easily embedded.

- **Policy and communications:** We must translate legal concerns into product features: e.g. a clear “Cancellation policy” notice on the booking form (to satisfy consumer rules), options for consent to marketing (GDPR). Also implement GDPR-friendly data handling (e.g. automatic purge of old reservations, simple opt-out). The module should log customer consent to terms and possibly double-opt-in for reminders if needed.

- **Minimum viable set:** Given uncertainties, we might leave more advanced ideas (e.g. AI phone booking) out of initial scope. Instead, we should focus on basics with well-documented behavior: reservations entry/edit, availability blocking, email/SMS reminders, simple deposit processing, and easy cancellation. Further hypotheses (like dynamic pricing or AI seat optimization) should remain tentative features pending evidence.

In summary, M0.3 should clarify which features are **mandatory (must-have)** vs experimental. It should specify how the module will obtain and validate required input (tables, hours, customer data), and how it will communicate with guests. Also, we need contingency: if a hypothesis fails (e.g. deposit uptake), we have fallback scenarios (e.g. making deposit optional or off by default).
