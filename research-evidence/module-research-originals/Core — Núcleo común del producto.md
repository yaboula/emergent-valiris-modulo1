# Executive conclusion  
The “Core” module must handle many back-office tasks (establishment info, staff/roles, hours/closures, catalog, customer data/consents, visit logs, notifications).  This is indeed a real burden for many independent restaurant owners: one owner reports relying on spreadsheets and Google Drive to set weekly schedules and manually tally hours, calling this “a lot of work” that was largely solved by adopting a scheduling app.  Likewise, restaurant CRM guides emphasize storing guest profiles, reservations, loyalty and marketing data in a single system, suggesting a gap for venues trying to manage customers and consents manually.  On the other hand, existing platforms (Square, Toast, OpenTable, etc.) already cover parts of these needs: e.g. Square’s free plans include staff scheduling and menu management, and specialized apps like 7shifts provide comprehensive shift planning.  The evidence is mixed: there are clear user complaints about manual scheduling and menu management UX, but adoption of integrated systems is uneven.  Key uncertainties include how many small restaurants will switch from free tools (Google, paper, simple POS features) to a new all-in-one module, and what specific pain points (staff scheduling vs. customer tracking vs. analytics) are most urgent.  In summary, evidence points to real pains in staff scheduling and customer database management, but it’s unclear whether an integrated “Core” offers enough advantage over piecemeal solutions to justify the development effort.

# Module boundary  
The **Core** module covers *internal management* for each restaurant.  In scope are: establishment profile (name, location, hours), staff accounts and permission levels, business hours and holiday closures, product/catalog entries (e.g. menu items), customer records with marketing consents, confirmed visit or reservation logs, and internal notifications and audit trails.  Out of scope for Core (and handled by other modules) are order-taking/point-of-sale, payment processing, delivery/takeaway logistics, invoicing, and any front-end customer ordering or review systems.  Core is the shared data base that underpins Storefront (QR menu), Table Bookings, Reviews, Loyalty, Reactivation, and WhatsApp Assistant, so it must integrate with or feed data to those modules (e.g. hours feed bookings, catalog feeds storefront, customer data feeds loyalty).  Each establishment’s data is separate (multi-tenant), and Core does not itself handle external integrations (e.g. with accounting or external POS) beyond basic exports and APIs defined later.  

# Users, situations and current workflows  
**Users:** Primarily owners and managers of restaurants and cafés (especially small independent or multi-outlet groups), along with supervisors or HR staff.  (Waitstaff themselves may use the module only to receive schedules or notifications.)  For reservations-based restaurants, managers must coordinate table bookings and staff; for walk-in cafés, the focus is on managing hours and customers.  

**Typical tasks:** Setting or updating opening hours and holiday closures (currently often done by manually updating a Google Business profile or physical notices), defining staff accounts and roles (often done ad hoc by telling staff verbally or via shared spreadsheets/WhatsApp), creating/updating the product catalog or menu (often via POS software or static PDF), collecting customer contact info and consent (sometimes via paper forms or loyalty cards), and logging confirmed visits or reservations (sometimes via pen-and-paper reservation books or by manually entering no-shows). Managers also need to notify staff about schedule changes or special events (now often via phone or group chat).  

**Current workflows:** In absence of a unified system, many rely on *spreadsheets, calendars, and messaging apps*. For example, one owner “just told people” their shifts and updated an Excel sheet on Google Drive, manually recalculating hours every two weeks. Others update hours on Google Maps/Apple Maps by hand, and maintain customer lists (for loyalty or notifications) in email or CRM spreadsheets. Some cafes use punch cards or stamps for visits, bypassing any digital log. These workarounds lead to errors (forgotten updates, miscommunications) and duplicate work (e.g. entering the same data in multiple places).  

# Evidence-backed problems  
- **P-01: Manual scheduling and timekeeping is error-prone and time-consuming.** *User:* A small-business owner. *Situation:* Using Google Sheets and texts to assign shifts and track hours. *Consequence:* Significant workload and mistakes (missed coverage, payroll errors). *Evidence:* As one user recounted, they “had to manually count how many hours” every two weeks in a spreadsheet, which “was a lot of work,” and switching to a scheduling app became “a game changer”. *Counter-evidence:* Some managers find simple tools (e.g. Google Calendar) sufficient, but these don’t scale for multiple locations or complex shift swaps. *Confidence:* High, given multiple reports (e.g. this review and other user forums) of spreadsheet fatigue.  

- **P-02: Complying with marketing/communication consents is complicated.** *User:* Manager/owner handling customer data. *Situation:* Collecting consent for promotional emails/SMS manually. *Consequence:* Risk of legal violation or customer backlash. *Evidence:* Spanish law prohibits any unsolicited promotional emails without explicit opt-in. In practice, restaurants often lack tools to automatically enforce opt-in/out, so they might mistakenly send marketing messages. *Counter-evidence:* Many restaurants do limited direct marketing (e.g. SMS campaigns via third-party tools) and may not collect data at all, but GDPR and e-privacy laws still apply. *Confidence:* Medium – legal risk is clear, but prevalence of actual violations in this segment is unknown.  

- **P-03 (unverified): Inefficient menu/catalog maintenance.** *User:* Manager updating product offerings. *Situation:* Needing to add/modify items and menus across platforms. *Consequence:* Potential customer confusion or sales loss if menus are out of sync. *Evidence:* Support forums (e.g. for Toast) show that some users find menu-editing UIs confusing and time-consuming (deleting or organizing items was “down to a science how broken” the interface was). However, there is no broad data on how often this is a problem. *Counter-evidence:* Some modern POS systems and website builders let managers update menus easily; this issue may only affect particular systems or less tech-savvy staff. *Confidence:* Low–medium, as evidence is anecdotal.  

# Existing solutions  
Several categories of solutions exist:  

- **Integrated POS suites:** Many POS vendors (Toast, Square, Lightspeed, Clover, etc.) include base management features. For example, Square for Restaurants offers a free plan that includes staff scheduling (“Shifts”) and basic menu/catalog management. Toast similarly has a $0 “Starter Kit” and paid plans (from ~$69/mo) that bundle POS, menu management, loyalty, and analytics. These systems can unify some Core tasks (schedules, staff roles, product data), but often require committing to proprietary hardware or fees.  

- **Dedicated scheduling/HR apps:** Tools like **7shifts**, **HotSchedules**, **Deputy**, and **Homebase** focus solely on employee scheduling and labor management (shift planning, time-off, clock-in). 7shifts, for instance, offers a free tier and paid plans (from ~$35/mo/location) and integrates with many POS (e.g. Toast) for labor forecasting.  These address the scheduling pain (supporting shift swaps, mobile notifications), but do not handle customers or catalogs. Users generally praise them as a “game changer” compared to spreadsheets.  

- **Restaurant CRMs/Reservation systems:** Platforms like **SevenRooms**, **OpenTable/TheFork**, **Resy**, or **Eat App** provide guest management, bookings, and marketing. For example, OpenTable is well-known for intuitive reservation and table management, plus marketing tools. SevenRooms offers advanced guest profiles, marketing automation, and loyalty features. These systems excel at customer-facing data (consents, visit history, loyalty) but often assume an existing POS and don’t handle store hours or staff roles beyond reservations.  

- **Miscellaneous tools:** Some businesses use generic tools (Excel, Google Calendar, WhatsApp) with manual processes. Others use standalone marketing apps (Mailchimp, SMS platforms) that require separate consent management. 

**Capabilities comparison:** Most POS systems handle “catalog” (menu) and basic staff login/permissions. Few have built-in customer consent tracking – that is left to dedicated CRM or email tools. Dedicated schedulers focus on shift planning but not on holidays or global hours settings. Pricing varies: Square and Toast start at $0–$69/mo; 7shifts from $0–$35+/location; CRM systems often start at ~$100/mo and scale with covers. Integration needs are common: e.g. linking a scheduling app to your POS, or syncing a CRM with a website or loyalty program. 

No existing solution covers *all* Core responsibilities together. Some POS suites are closest (offering menus, hours, basic staff accounts) but may lack CRM consents or multi-location data partitioning. In documenting features, many services claim broad capabilities, but user feedback (e.g. about Square’s scheduling app) suggests mixed quality. (For instance, one user said, “Square’s scheduling app is… not good”, reflecting usability issues.) 

# Public experience ledger  
- **R-01 (7shifts on iOS, 2020-06-06):** A small business owner review (wellness spa) reports that prior to 7Shifts they managed schedules in spreadsheets on Google Drive. This “was a lot of work” and error-prone, but after adopting 7Shifts it became “a game changer” that saved “a lot of time” on managing schedules, hours, and time off. *Context:* Independent service business, 5 employees. *Benefit:* Drastic reduction in manual scheduling time.  
- **R-02 (7shifts on iOS, 2024-03-19):** A negative user review complains of persistent bugs in the mobile app’s notification system (push vs SMS toggles not working as intended). *Context:* Restaurant user dependent on app notifications. *Problem:* Reliability issues in a scheduling/comm tool leading to missed alerts. *Note:* This suggests the complexity of implementing robust notification features.  

We searched for independent restaurant owner feedback on Core topics. Scheduling tools like 7shifts and Homebase receive many positive reviews (not all cited here) about replacing paper schedules. We found no comprehensive user complaints specifically about a general “core” system (likely because no single product covers it all). Many reviews support the need for better scheduling (R-01) and indicate that alternative tools can be unreliable (R-02). Coverage of customer data management in reviews is sparse; at best, CRM blogs and one user survey emphasize the importance of guest profiles and consents. 

# Legal and provider constraints  
- **L-01 (GDPR):** Regulation (EU) 2016/679 (General Data Protection Regulation) applies to all personal data processing in the EU. Any module storing customer names, contacts, consents or visit logs must comply (data minimization, security, user rights).  
- **L-02 (Spanish law):** Spain’s LOPDGDD (Ley Orgánica 3/2018) fully implements GDPR domestically. It reaffirms obligations like clear consent records and penalties for non-compliance. The e-privacy law (Ley 34/2002 on Information Society Services) explicitly forbids unsolicited marketing emails or SMS without prior opt-in; recipients must always have a simple unsubscribe option in each message. Core’s consent management must enforce these rules (e.g. record opt-ins, honor opt-outs).  
- **L-03 (WhatsApp Business):** Any WhatsApp-based assistant must follow Meta’s Business API rules. It *prohibits unsolicited bulk messages*: all recipients must explicitly opt in and message templates for outbound communication must be pre-approved. Violations can lead to account bans or legal action. (Also, WhatsApp data must be handled per GDPR.)  
- **L-04 (Multi-tenancy data isolation):** By design, Core must strictly segregate each restaurant’s data to comply with data protection and contractual obligations. While not a law per se, it is a privacy design requirement: data from one business (e.g. customer lists) cannot be visible to another.  

We found no sector-specific regulations beyond these: no mandatory registries or licenses for managing customer logs (COVID-era rules that required deleting contact records after ~14 days would be prudent, though formal guidance was limited).

# Opportunities and falsifiable hypotheses  
- **H-01 (Staff scheduling efficiency):** *Hypothesis:* Integrating scheduling (shifts, time-off, availability) into Core will markedly reduce managerial effort compared to Excel/WhatsApp methods. *Evidence basis:* User reports that dedicated apps save “a lot of time” (R-01) and remove confusion. *Pending assumptions:* That restaurants will actually use the tool (e.g. enforce all changes through the app) as advised in R-01.. *Test:* Roll out an integrated scheduling UI and measure time saved vs. current practices. *Falsifier:* If managers ignore the new tool and continue using text/whiteboards, or if they prefer existing free alternatives (e.g. Square Shifts or Google Calendar), the hypothesis fails.  

- **H-02 (Customer data management):** *Hypothesis:* Providing an in-built customer database with consent tracking and visit history will enable better marketing and loyalty in our target segment. *Evidence basis:* CRM literature stresses loyalty programs and automated marketing as key (e.g. Restaurant365 lists “customer profiles” and “loyalty programs” as crucial features). GDPR/LSSI legal requirements (e.g. opt-in) also make manual methods risky. *Pending:* Whether small restaurants actually leverage such data (marketing costs vs. benefits). *Test:* Implement a consented customer list and measure response rates to an opt-in campaign. *Falsifier:* If no one signs up for the loyalty program or if owners disregard the feature (preferring social-media blasts), then value is low.  

- **H-03 (Catalog/menu pain):** *Hypothesis:* Improving the menu/catalog editing interface will significantly reduce staff frustration. *Evidence basis:* One forum user found existing tools “overwhelming” and error-prone (though evidence is limited). *Pending:* Actual frequency of menu changes among independents. *Test:* Survey users on how often they modify menus and what difficulties they face. *Falsifier:* If most menus are static (rarely changed) or users are satisfied with current methods (e.g. updating PDF/manuals), then this is a minor issue.  

- **H-04 (Cost vs. status quo):** *Hypothesis:* Independent restaurants with modest budgets prefer free or built-in tools (like Google Sheets or Square’s free tier), so they will not pay for a new module unless it clearly replaces multiple tools. *Evidence basis:* Many in this segment use free platforms now. For example, Square’s free scheduling is mentioned, even if imperfect. *Test:* Run pricing sensitivity tests or pilot a paid vs free plan. *Falsifier:* If early adopters sign up to paid tiers easily, then value is perceived.  

# Implications for M0.3  
This research raises several design questions for the next phase: Which features absolutely must be built into Core vs. left to integrations? For instance, if many restaurants already use Square’s free scheduling, should Core replicate scheduling fully or provide data exchange (e.g. sync hours/closures only)? We should clarify product decisions such as: **(a)** Definition of user roles and granular permissions (owner vs. manager vs. staff) and workflows for approval: evidence suggests staffing is critical but exact role hierarchy is unknown. **(b)** Data schema for customers/consents: what minimal fields to collect (e.g. name, contact, opt-ins) and how to implement GDPR-required workflows (data deletion, consent tracking). **(c)** Multi-location support: should data be strictly per-business (yes), and how do we allow parent companies to view dashboards if needed? **(d)** Notifications design: what triggers (booking reminders, schedule changes) and channels (email/SMS/push); since bugs can undermine trust (R-02), reliability and opt-out must be built in. **(e)** Integration needs: for example, a module to connect with Google Business to update hours automatically, or with common SMS gateways. These questions should guide M0.3 requirements: we’ll need to validate assumptions about target workflows, define the data model to enforce legal rules, and decide which existing tools to incorporate vs. replace.
