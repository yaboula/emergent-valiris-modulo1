# Executive conclusion  
Reactivating dormant diners appears to be a valuable but under‐served need for neighborhood restaurants. Evidence from restaurant tools (e.g. Toast’s “Miss You” campaigns) and user reports (17× ROI on a $2 coupon; \$500–\$1,000 revenue per email) show that targeted outreach can produce strong returns. However, skepticism remains about true lift (restaurants note that some customers would have returned anyway). Small operators often lack systematic processes (relying on spreadsheets or staff memory) and may not track consent properly, risking compliance issues. The strongest evidence supports automated, data-driven campaigns: industry guides emphasize RFM segmentation and multi-channel outreach (email+SMS), and platforms like Toast tie promotions to actual POS visits to verify results. Key uncertainties include how many local eateries even attempt reactivation (vs. ignoring inactives), what contact data they reliably capture, and how to measure true campaign impact on footfall. In summary, reactivation can solve the problem of lost repeat business for restaurant owners when done with customer consent, but its success depends on quality of visit data and careful measurement.  

# Module boundary  
We consider only **customer reactivation** – using past visit history and marketing communications to bring back lapsed customers.  We assume the core system provides customer profiles with visit dates and consent flags, and a channel (email/SMS/WhatsApp) for messaging.  We do *not* include point-of-sale or ordering functions, menu management, or general loyalty (points) programs beyond what is needed for identifying customers and recording visits.  Activities like taking reservations or serving the meal are out of scope.  We exclude unrelated marketing (e.g. broad social media ads) and focus on direct re-engagement of known customers who have not visited in their “normal” interval.  

# Users, situations and current workflows  
**Primary users:** Owners/managers and staff of independent local restaurants and cafes (both those with reservation systems and those relying on walk-ins), who seek to bring back lapsed customers.  Also marketing staff or consultants if used.  For reservation-based restaurants, past guest data may come from the reservation system; for walk-in cafes, from loyalty cards or guestbooks.  **Typical tasks:** Identify which customers have gone too long without a visit, craft and send them promotional messages (discounts, reminders), and then track if they return.  Restaurants must manage opt‑ins/opt‑outs for email/SMS to comply with consent laws.  They may segment customers (e.g. VIP vs casual) and decide timing (e.g. send a “we miss you” offer after 60 days of no-show).  **Current alternatives:** Many small venues use *manual* methods – e.g. staff hand-writing cards for regulars, basic loyalty punch cards, or Excel/contact lists.  Some try sending periodic newsletters or posts on social media.  Without a system, they may simply *call* or text familiar customers on a whim.  These approaches are labor-intensive and error-prone: spreadsheets capture only basic contact info, not visit frequency, so it’s hard to know who truly is “inactive.”  Larger chains might hire agencies or use generic CRM/email tools (Mailchimp, Sendinblue, etc.), but integration is ad hoc.  In short, operators often lack dedicated tools, relying on memory or ad-hoc efforts to reach out.  

# Evidence-backed problems  
- **P-01: Limited visibility into inactive customers (data gap).** *Who:* Small restaurateurs using manual records. *Situation:* They keep contact info but rarely record each visit. *Consequence:* They cannot accurately identify who qualifies as “inactive.” As one Spanish CRM vendor notes, a spreadsheet yields only an email, whereas a specialized system shows visit count and last visit date. Without that, owners miss chances to re-engage. *Confidence:* High, given this gap in current workflows. *Contraevidence:* None found; some tools (Toast) offer this view automatically.  

- **P-02: Manual effort is time-consuming and inconsistent.** *Who:* Restaurant owners/managers. *Situation:* Staff must export, filter and message lists by hand, often balancing this with many other duties. *Consequence:* Campaigns happen infrequently or not at all. For example, a user noted having thousands of contacts but “was REALLY bad at sending things to that list before”. This suggests a real obstacle. *Confidence:* Medium; anecdotal evidence (R-02) and the fact that restaurants generally lack dedicated marketers. *Contraevidence:* Some use integrated tools to automate (e.g. Toast campaign triggers), but small users may not.  

- **P-03: Weak attribution – opens ≠ actual visits.** *Who:* Marketing staff/owners. *Situation:* They send emails/SMS hoping customers come back. *Consequence:* It’s hard to tell if any uptick in visits was caused by the campaign. As one reviewer remarked, common ROI reporting may count any visit after an email, ignoring that guests might have returned anyway. This uncertainty undermines confidence in the tool. *Evidence:* Toast cites a ~39% average open rate, but open/click does not guarantee a sale. A user explicitly warned that attributing every return to an email overstates impact. *Confidence:* High; consistent with marketing lore. *Contraevidence:* Sophisticated platforms tie promotions to POS data (Toast’s integrated suite), but even then causality is hard to prove.  

- **P-04: Consent/legal compliance burdens.** *Who:* Owners using email/SMS. *Situation:* They must only contact customers who opted in. *Consequence:* Without clear consent records and unsubscribe mechanisms, they risk violating Spanish/EU law. Spanish LSSI prohibits unsolicited commercial emails without prior express permission, and mandates easy opt-out. Restaurants may not realize an email collected for reservations isn’t auto-eligible for marketing. *Confidence:* High, given the strict law. *Contraevidence:* None; this is a regulatory fact.  

- **P-05: Maintenance of up-to-date customer status.** *Who:* Staff tracking campaigns. *Situation:* Campaigns should skip unsubscribed or no-shows, but manual lists quickly become stale. *Consequence:* Risk sending unwanted messages or missing valid targets. *Evidence:* LSSI/LOPD require honoring opt-outs, and real experience shows many unsub per campaign if lists are not cleansed. One user cleaned his list to only those with 6+ visits. *Confidence:* Medium. *Contraevidence:* Automated tools often handle unsubscribes (Toast maintains lists), but only if customers use them.

**Specialized marketing suites** – Large hotel or franchise systems (e.g. Oracle Hospitality CRM, Salesforce Hospitality Cloud) can segment and automate campaigns across channels. These are enterprise-grade and costly (often ≥€300/month/location). They usually require integration of reservation, dining and loyalty data. *Unverified:* Whether any segment by “visit gap” exists out of box (likely customizable).  

**Summary of gaps:** No fully turnkey, Spanish-language tool for small restaurants is widely established. Most offerings are either US-centric or general-purpose. Many features (e.g. linking campaign to actual visit credit) remain “unverified” for our use case without evidence.

# Legal and provider constraints  
- **L-01 (LSSI 34/2002, Art.21):** Spanish law forbids sending promotional emails/SMS without prior *solicited or expressly authorized* consent. Restaurants cannot send blast promotions to customers who only received transactional emails (e.g. reservation confirmations) unless those customers explicitly opted in. Any campaign must respect an opt-in consent record. Additionally, each message must offer a free unsubscribe link (even SMS must allow opt-out). *Applicability:* Nationwide Spain, ePrivacy directive implementation; in effect since 2002 (amended 2014).  
- **L-02 (GDPR/LOPDGDD):** EU/Spanish data protection law requires lawful basis (for marketing, it’s usually explicit consent) to process personal contact data. Data collected (names, phone numbers, emails, visit history) must be kept only as long as needed for reactivation purposes. Customers have rights to access and delete their data; the system must allow fulfilling such requests. Special care: tracking “visits” could be seen as profiling; customers must be informed. *Applicability:* EU-wide (GDPR, since May 2018), plus Spanish adaptation LOPDGDD.  
- **L-03 (WhatsApp policies):** If using WhatsApp Business for reactivation, one must use *message templates* approved by WhatsApp, and only message users who have opted in to receive WhatsApp messages. This is provider policy (not law), but violations can lead to bans. No Spanish special law, but note it.  
- **L-04 (SMS regulation):** Bulk SMS marketing is also regulated under LSSI as “equivalent electronic communications.” Unsolicited SMS campaigns require prior consent, and each SMS must allow opt-out (often via reply STOP).  

Each constraint demands that the reactivation module include consent capture and revocation flows. Non-compliance (sending without consent or without unsubscribe) risks fines under 34/2002 and GDPR (which can be significant, though small businesses sometimes escape heavy scrutiny).

# Opportunities and falsifiable hypotheses  
- **H-01:** *Automated reactivation raises revisit rate.*  Rationale: users report high campaign ROI. We hypothesize that sending personalized “we miss you” offers to lapsed customers increases their short-term visit probability by >10% (versus no campaign).  *Assumption:* Customers truly lapsed. *To falsify:* If subsequent visits among emailed customers is no better than an unemailed control group, this fails. Also if unsubscribes are high, motivation is low.  
- **H-02:** *Segmenting by past frequency improves efficiency.*  Rationale: experienced marketers only message customers with a history of frequent visits. Hypothesis: targeting only customers above some visit-count threshold yields better ROI (higher conversion per message) than a blanket approach. *Test:* Compare return rates for segments; disconfirm if low-frequency past buyers respond equally well.  
- **H-03:** *SMS+Email (multichannel) is more effective than single channel.*  Rationale: Marketing guides suggest multichannel improves reach. We hypothesize that adding SMS reminders to an email campaign will yield a measurable uplift in responses. *Falsify:* If SMS yields little extra visits or annoyance (high unsub), then the added complexity isn’t justified.  
- **H-04:** *Without confirmed visit tracking, attribution overstates impact.*  Rationale: users warned ROI can be inflated. Hypothesis: Only customers with a logged visit (POS entry, QR scan, or reservation check-in) count as true reactivations. If we ignore this, claimed lift may disappear. To test: link each redeemed promotion to an actual recorded visit. *Observation that refutes:* If we see many sales on coupons without matching reservation or POS entries, our visit data is incomplete (and the campaign’s “success” is suspect).  
- **H-05:** *Lack of reactivation tool is common in segment.*  Rationale: We found few Spanish solutions, implying many restaurants do nothing systematic. Hypothesis: A majority (>50%) of target restaurants currently have no automated reactivation process and would welcome a simple tool. *To check:* Survey/restaurateur interviews needed. *Falsifier:* If evidence emerges that most use some marketing email tool or fear legal risk and avoid it, then the opportunity is smaller.

# Implications for M0.3  
The product definition must clarify: **Data dependencies:** need accurate customer visit records (from bookings or loyalty scans) and reliable contact info (email/SMS consent flagged). The module should integrate with the core’s customer directory (to pull visit dates and consent status) and with external messaging services. We must specify how “confirmed visit” is defined (e.g. reservation used or loyalty point earned) to avoid counting casual web clicks. Unverified assumption: QR menu usage or table bookings supply visit logs; we must confirm this data exists.  

Key questions to resolve next phase: *How to segment “inactive”?* (e.g. X days since last visit vs average frequency). *What channels to support?* (email is common, SMS and possibly WhatsApp?). *Consent capture:* likely need to record opt-ins at sign-up or on receipts. *Campaign flow:* Will owners manually trigger a campaign or have it auto-send when thresholds are crossed? Should test both. 

We should also consider design: offer “preview of list and send” screens, and standard templates (e.g. birthday vs generic “miss you”). The evidence warns not to oversell features already provided by the core loyalty system (like stamping points). Instead, focus on “if no one came for X days, send Y”. Before committing requirements, we must verify how reliable the core data is (e.g. are walk-in visits recorded? Are guests identified or anonymous?). Finally, ensure legal compliance features: require consent status checks and unsubscribe processing before any campaign is allowed.
