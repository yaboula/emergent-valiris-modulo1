# Executive conclusion  
The **Reviews** module would solve the challenge of systematically gathering and managing customer feedback in independent cafés and restaurants.  Currently, owners rely on ad-hoc methods (e.g. flyers, verbally asking, QR codes on receipts) and must manually monitor Google/TripAdvisor, so many positive experiences go undocumented while issues can go unnoticed or be vented publicly.  We found strong guidance but little hard data. Google explicitly **forbids** incentivizing or selectively soliciting reviews, and Spanish law requires *prior consent* for any customer contact. Some specialized tools exist (e.g. Tableo, LocalImpact, Reviewflowz) that automate invitations via email/SMS/WhatsApp and aggregate reviews, indicating that modest conversion rates (~5–8%) are achievable.  Anecdotal evidence shows managers find multi-platform solicitation “**way too much**” for customers and advise using **QR code** pages to let users pick a preferred review site.  The strongest evidence is vendor-provided (e.g. claimed 7.5% conversion) and best-practice advice. Key uncertainties remain: **Will customers actually respond to digital review invitations, and can we prevent or handle the resulting negative feedback?** For example, one owner described a malicious “review-bomb” threat from a social media user. Our hypotheses (e.g. that automated invites significantly boost review counts) must be tested. In summary, there is a credible need to automate invitations and feedback collection, but we must respect legal constraints and Google policy, and validate customer behavior.

# Module boundary  
This research covers only the **Reviews** module as defined (post-visit feedback, review invitations, and response preparation).  It excludes order/delivery/takeaway systems, POS, invoicing, and unrelated CRM.  We focus on *collecting post-visit feedback and Google reviews* via invitations, and helping the manager spot and respond to issues.  Out of scope: building a full reservation system or multi-channel marketing suite.  We assume core data (visits, customer contacts, consent) is provided by the platform core.  We do not design technical architecture or choose stack here.

# Users, situations and current workflows  
**Users.** The primary users are small restaurant or café owners/managers (often the same person in independent venues), plus any staff involved in guest relations. The module also interacts with **diners** (customers who may receive invites). Owners want to improve reputation and fix problems; staff may handle day-to-day guest follow-up. In our segment, establishments may have reservations (sit-down restaurants) or be mostly walk-in (cafés/bars).  

**Situations.** Inviting reviews happens *immediately after a visit*. In restaurants with bookings, the system could auto-send an email/SMS to the guest. In walk-in cafés, usually no contact is recorded, so businesses today rely on *in-store prompts*: e.g. printing a QR code on the bill or a table sign. For example, one business simply prints “scan this QR code to leave us a Google review” on their receipt. Feedback also arises if a customer had a problem and complains (either directly or via a “report card”).  

**Current workflows.** Without this module, restaurants use manual or point solutions:  
- **Verbal asks**: Staff may (awkwardly) ask satisfied customers to review on Google or TripAdvisor, but many feel this is uncomfortable for both sides.  
- **Printed QR/link**: Some put a static QR code or short URL on the table or receipt linking to their Google review page. This automates the link but still relies on customers scanning it.  
- **Email campaigns**: A few more tech-savvy owners send a follow-up email survey or thank-you message (if they collected email) with a review link. This is labor-intensive (copying addresses, writing emails) and must obey spam laws (opt-in required).  
- **No system**: Many rely on “organic” reviews (customers find them and leave feedback spontaneously) or just on-site observation of guest mood. Negative incidents often only surface if the customer *elsewhere* posts a scathing Google review.  

No standard solution is universal. Some third-party tools (like Tableo) exist but are not widely adopted. There is no built-in process to both capture private complaints and publicly solicit reviews **in compliance with policy**.  

# Evidence-backed problems  

**P-01: Low review volume and trust-building.** *Users*: Owners/managers. *Situation*: Customers rarely leave reviews unless prompted, so small venues have few reviews. *Consequence*: Without social proof, new customers may doubt the business; it’s harder to rank in search. *Evidence*: The need for reviews is asserted by vendors – e.g. Tableo’s site emphasizes that “displaying reviews motivates new diners to choose your restaurant”. This implies that without visible reviews, customer trust and conversions suffer. While concrete adoption stats are lacking, the emphasis on reviews as “trust signals” suggests a real problem. *Counter-evidence*: Many small businesses survive without many reviews; a few locals may know them personally. We lack data on how common review-seeking is. *Confidence*: Medium (supported by industry claims, but no independent stats).

**P-02: Soliciting reviews is cumbersome and awkward.** *Users*: Owners/managers, staff. *Situation*: Asking a customer to review is often seen as a favor; asking across multiple platforms feels “way too much”. *Consequence*: Many owners either avoid asking (missing potential reviews) or only on a whim, so review collection is inconsistent. *Evidence*: A small-business owner reported on Reddit that while Google reviews are feasible, asking customers to sign up and review on Trustpilot, Facebook, etc., felt like an excessive burden. Advice was to use a **single QR code** page listing options – highlighting the friction of current methods. This anecdote shows owners struggle with the process. *Counter-evidence*: Some venues may have workflows (e.g. handing out cards) that work for them. Also, QR code use (e.g.) can mitigate awkwardness. *Confidence*: Medium (based on user comment; may not generalize to all customers or cultures).

**P-03: Risk of negative reviews and lack of private feedback.** *Users*: Diners (unhappy customers) and managers. *Situation*: Every venue will have the occasional complaint; if customers only post publicly (e.g. on Google) without manager knowledge, issues escalate. *Consequence*: A few bad reviews can disproportionately harm rating and reputation. Worse, malicious actors can exploit reviews: one restaurant owner was threatened with a “review bomb” via TikTok unless he paid off the customer. *Evidence*: The TikTok incident (R-01) is an extreme case where a stranger faked an incident to blackmail the owner with negative publicity. Even without malice, customers may vent frustrations publicly if there’s no easy private outlet. *Counter-evidence*: Google reviews are moderated and people with genuine complaints might still give fair feedback. However, many businesses lament that only satisfied customers will post unless there’s a simple channel for critics. *Confidence*: Medium (one vivid example and general logic, but we lack prevalence data).

**P-04: Legal and policy constraints hinder incentives.** *Users*: Owners/marketing. *Situation*: Managers want to boost reviews but cannot “select only happy customers” or offer discounts for reviews. *Consequence*: They cannot legally or by policy “game” the system. For example, Google explicitly forbids giving rewards for reviews. Spanish law (LSSI) forbids unsolicited marketing emails without consent. *Evidence*: Google’s policy states offering any incentive in exchange for reviews is considered “fake engagement” and strictly forbidden. It also forbids selectively soliciting only positive reviews. Spanish Law 34/2002 requires prior consent for promotional emails and a clear unsubscribe option. These rules limit how restaurants can legally contact customers or motivate reviews. *Counter-evidence*: None – these are clear regulations. *Confidence*: High (direct official sources).

# Existing solutions  
A few specialized products address review collection, but none dominate the small-restaurant segment in Spain:

- **Tableo (Spain)** – A Spanish startup (see Tableo’s website) that lets restaurants send automated email/SMS invitations with custom survey questions. It advertises *“Envía automáticamente un email o SMS al día siguiente de su visita”*. Tableo tracks ratings on food/service and can publish favorable reviews on the restaurant’s website. Pricing is not public, but likely subscription-based. Integration likely requires manually importing customer contacts or syncing with a POS.

- **LocalImpact** – A cloud service ($19/month) that automates review requests. According to its listing, it “send[s] automated review requests by SMS, email, or direct links” to solicit Google and other reviews. It tracks incoming reviews and provides AI-assisted response suggestions. It also offers widgets to display reviews on the business website. It’s web-based, aimed at multi-location businesses, and has review aggregation (Google, Yelp, etc.). No free plan (though a trial may exist). Lower-end for small clients ($19). It requires entering a customer list or using an API from booking software.

- **Reviewflowz (for NowBookIt)** – A review management tool oriented to hospitality (price tier starting $19/mo after trial). For example, its site claims: “Reviewflowz triggers review requests immediately when guests check out… Choose email ($5/1000), SMS ($5/100), or WhatsApp… Our customers average 7.5% conversion.” It syncs with reservation data (NowBookIt) to tag reviews with staff. It even automates staff attribution: if guests leave a 5-star review on Google/Yelp, Reviewflowz sends that data back so the server gets credit (without guests naming the server) – a policy-compliant way to know who made it. Reviewflowz also advertises AI-generated responses and analytics (percent conversion, etc.). It’s more expensive ($19+/mo + per-invite costs) and aimed at English-language markets. 

- **“Rize” Reviews** – A managed service ($199/month) that will “do everything for you” to get positive reviews. It solicits feedback via email/text/Kiosk and even prints “review us” cards. It focuses on major review sites. This is more of a full-service agency (responses written by humans) than a self-serve tool.

- **Birdeye/Podium** – US enterprise tools (hundreds $/month) not tailored to tiny restaurants. Birdeye offers similar multi-channel review requests and monitoring; Podium is SMS/chat-focused.

- **Google Business Tools** – For free, Google lets any business generate a short “Leave us a review” link or QR code. Owners can manually send this (e.g. via Gmail or printouts), but there is no automated reminder feature out of the box.

In summary, solutions typically provide:
- **Automated invitations** (email/SMS/WhatsApp) after a visit.
- **Multi-platform review aggregation** (Google, Yelp, etc.).
- **Analytics**: conversion rates (e.g. Reviewflowz shows reviews / customers), trends.
- **Response management**: alerts and AI-suggested replies.
- **Integrations**: with booking/CRM (as with Reviewflowz) or via generic APIs.

**Conditions and limitations** vary: Reviewflowz charges per invite, LocalImpact has a flat rate, Tableo is tailored to Spanish businesses (presumably in €, with no public pricing). All require customer contact data. WhatsApp channels boast high open rates (Reviewflowz notes 98% WhatsApp open vs 20% email). 

No competitor appears to offer a truly free, policy-compliant all-in-one (the Google tools are free but manual; others are paid). Features advertised do **not** prove actual effectiveness; each tool’s claims (like 7.5% conversion) lack independent verification. Where a capability cannot be verified (e.g. automated negative-issue handling), we mark it “unverified.”

# Legal and provider constraints  

- **Google policies:** As noted, *must not* provide incentives for reviews or restrict invitations to only happy customers. Google calls such behavior “fake engagement.” It allows soliciting reviews only as long as it represents a genuine customer experience. These are binding if we use Google’s platform. (Policy updates should be checked regularly.)

- **Spain – Electronic communications (LSSI-CE, Ley 34/2002):** Email or SMS invitations count as “comunicaciones comerciales.” They require *prior consent* except when there is a prior customer relationship. If using a customer’s contact info, the business must have obtained it lawfully and provided an easy unsubscribe option. Translated: “sending advertising or promotional emails that were not expressly requested or authorized by the recipient is prohibited”, and every message must include a one-click way to opt-out. Violating these is a serious infraction under Art.38 LSSI. SMS/WhatsApp follow similar rules (opt-in required, plus adhere to telecom spam laws). 

- **Data protection (GDPR/LOPDGDD):** Collecting customer emails or phone numbers and sending messages is processing personal data. We must have a legal basis (likely *legitimate interest* or explicit consent) and follow GDPR principles (purpose limitation, transparency, data minimization). This implies informing customers their contact might be used for feedback requests and honoring their right to withdraw consent (along with LSSI unsubscribe). 

- **Advertising law:** Spanish law (Real Decreto Legislativo 1/2007 and Ley 34/1988 on advertising) forbids misleading advertising. Fabricated reviews or failing to disclose commercial motives in testimonials would violate the ban on “engañosa” or “desleal” advertising. While our module *encourages honest* customer reviews, any suggestion of “gating” reviews (asking only happy ones) or hiding incentives must be avoided to stay lawful. Officially, endorsements paid for or coerced must be clearly identified as advertising (not an issue if we forbid incentives). 

- **Miscellaneous:** If we integrate with WhatsApp, we must comply with WhatsApp Business policies (e.g. no automated spam). If including any auto-response (like chatbot), EU ePrivacy rules apply.

In all, compliance means (a) ensure customers have opted-in for follow-up, (b) never filter requests based on satisfaction, (c) never offer discounts for reviews, (d) allow opt-out, and (e) clearly label any automated messages as coming from the business. These requirements apply to Spain and broadly EU operations.

# Opportunities and falsifiable hypotheses  

- **Opportunity 1:** **Low-hanging improvements.** Some needs seem largely unaddressed: e.g. walk-in cafés with no contact info have no feedback channel today. Providing a simple QR feedback card or tablet could open a dialogue. Hypothesis H-01: *“Offering an in-store QR code that leads to a combined feedback/review portal (with buttons for Google or private feedback) will significantly increase participation.”*  Evidence from [103]: customers resist multi-step sign-ups, so a one-scan solution should help. We can falsify by measuring QR scans vs previous feedback.

- **Opportunity 2:** **Multichannel invites.** Email alone yields few responses (industry ~20% open). WhatsApp shows ~98% read rates. Hypothesis H-02: *“Including WhatsApp (and SMS) review invitations will more than triple response rates compared to email alone.”*  We have Reviewflowz’s claim as prior (7.5% total conversion via all channels). We should test email vs WhatsApp open/review rates. A refutation would be if WhatsApp invites annoy customers or get blocked.

- **Opportunity 3:** **Pre-public “satisfaction survey”**. The Reddit suggestion was to give unhappy customers a private feedback route. Hypothesis H-03: *“A brief post-visit survey (e.g. 1–5 star rating) routed internally will catch most dissatisfied customers and lead to fewer public negative reviews.”*  This yields better issue resolution. To test: compare public negative review rate with vs without the private survey link. If negative reviews stay the same or customers skip the survey, hypothesis fails.

- **Opportunity 4:** **Staff attribution/management.** Automatically tagging which server took care of a 5-star review (as Reviewflowz does) could improve staff motivation without violating Google’s no-mention rule. Hypothesis H-04: *“Automated staff attribution from reviews will increase manager engagement (more review replies) and staff satisfaction, without affecting review authenticity.”*  It’s falsifiable by A/B testing: managers with attribution respond more often to reviews than those without.

- **Opportunity 5:** **Targeted invitations.** Given Google’s ban on “only happy customers,” one idea is to send invites to *all* customers but smartly tailor messaging. Hypothesis H-05: *“Even customers who had middling satisfaction (e.g. 3–4 stars) will respond positively to a review invite if it’s phrased neutrally.”*  We’d check if triggering invites only on 5-star events vs 4-star yields different outcomes. A refuting observation would be that we only get reviews when we would have anyway.

Any identified opportunity comes with a counter-risk. For example, if inviting all customers equally (to stay policy-compliant), we might invite very unhappy guests who will post poor reviews. This risk must be weighed – perhaps mitigated by giving them an internal feedback form instead of going straight to Google. Hypotheses H-03 and H-05 above test this trade-off.

# Implications for M0.3  
Based on these findings, key questions for the next phase (product definition) include: 

- **Data and consents:** How will we capture/verify customer consent to contact them? The core must store opt-in status. We need clear consent flows (e.g. at booking or Wi-Fi login) before sending review invites. 

- **Communication channels:** Which channels to implement? Email is basic, but evidence suggests adding SMS/WhatsApp (with cost) could greatly improve reach. We must decide if we require integration with an SMS gateway or a WhatsApp Business API. 

- **Invite timing and content:** When to send invites? (e.g. immediately vs next day). What content to include? For compliance, we must avoid any promotional tone. Possibly we should send a neutral thank-you with a link. We also need an unsubscribe link in emails to satisfy LSSI.

- **Private feedback loop:** Will we include a pre-public survey step (like Net Promoter Score or a simple rating)? If so, how to act on it? We could require a manager to call back unsatisfied diners. Requirements: UI for staff to triage low scores and decide to escalate or invite to resolve.

- **Integration with core:** The module needs *visit records* (date, channel, guest contact info) from core. For table bookings, the email/phone is known; for walk-ins, maybe staff have to enter an email at payment. Or rely solely on QR codes for walk-ins. This dependency should be spelled out.

- **Google integration:** The product should interface with Google Business Profile (via API or link generation). Probably we just generate the correct review link; we cannot post reviews ourselves (user must submit).

- **Review monitoring & responses:** After reviews are posted publicly, managers might want to respond. Should our module aggregate incoming reviews from Google/TripAdvisor and alert the owner? This was outside the original scope but is a natural extension (many competitor tools do it). 

- **Performance metrics:** We should measure invite-to-review conversion rate, changes in average rating, no-show (when a negative score is given privately, do they not post publicly?), and perhaps differences in ROI between email vs SMS. Hypotheses above guide these metrics.

- **Policy compliance enforcement:** The design must enforce Google’s rules (e.g. by not highlighting only 5-star guests). We should require that *all* guests are treated equally by default. If we consider gating (some tools do “happy path vs unhappy path”), we must ensure it doesn’t violate Google or mislead customers.

- **User experience:** Avoid over-soliciting: possibly allow restaurants to set a maximum frequency or a “quiet period”. We should also make leaving a review as frictionless as possible (e.g. one-click via mobile). Based on [103] feedback, a QR code that leads to a multi-choice page might be needed if we invite to multiple platforms. (Though core market is Spain, where Google predominates.)

These questions and requirements should be explored in M0.3. **We have not made final decisions**; rather, we outline the issues that need validation through user research or prototyping. For instance, we must test whether customers react well to automated invites, and whether managers will correctly use a private feedback funnel (H-03). Without those answers, we cannot confidently specify feature requirements.
