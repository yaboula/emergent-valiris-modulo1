# Executive conclusion  
Restaurants increasingly need to provide up‐to‐date menus and information online. EU law now **requires** listing allergens on menus, and customers (especially tourists) expect digital access and translations.  However, most small independent eateries still use printed menus or static PDFs, leading to high reprint costs and stale information.  Dedicated digital menu tools (like QRMenu, OddMenu, iMenuPro, Menutech) have sprung up to solve this, each with free or low-cost plans (e.g. QRMenu’s free tier or OddMenu’s ~$10/mo). These tools tout easy editing, unlimited items, translations and analytics, and claim higher sales from “easy upsell”. Meanwhile even free services like Google Business Profile let restaurants upload full menus and photos. Our investigation finds that **most needs (basic menu publication, allergens, languages)** are already addressed by existing solutions and platforms, so the opportunity is not obvious. Key uncertainties remain about our specific segment: will **small local eateries** adopt another system if free options exist? Will staff actually maintain it (versus one‐off efforts)? We see evidence of benefits (reduced printing, better visibility) but also against it (non-technical staff, customers who dislike QR).  In summary, the strongest motivations for this module are compliance (allergen info) and multilingual accessibility, but ample alternatives exist. The main **unanswered question** is whether our target customers have enough unmet need (and motivation to switch) to justify building this module.  

# Module boundary  
This module covers **public-facing information pages and digital menus**. It must allow a restaurant to publish its name, address, hours, and a fully detailed menu (dishes, variants, prices, availability, translations and allergens), accessible via a unique link or QR code. It **excludes** any ordering, payment, table reservations or point-of-sale features (those belong to other modules). It also excludes internal restaurant operations (inventory, kitchens) and only concerns “written” menu content.  It **does not** handle invoicing, delivery logistics or loyalty; those are outside v1. Essentially it is a modern “virtual menu & info page” (a second storefront) that staff can update online.  

# Users, situations and current workflows  
**Primary users:** Restaurant owners/managers and staff maintain the menu content; customers (diners) and potential visitors (online searchers) view it.  In practice, an owner or kitchen manager must update prices and daily specials, ensure legal info (allergens) is correct, and the QR codes or links in the venue point to this content. Staff often have no extra time or tech skills, so simplicity is crucial.  

**Situations:**  A customer walks in (often tourists or locals) and wants to see the menu *before* ordering; they scan a QR code or click a link. Or a person researching before visiting (e.g. on Google or social media) wants to find the menu, hours, and location.  Restaurants that take reservations may also put the menu link on reservation sites.  For small cafés with walk-in customers, paper menus have been replaced by QR systems during COVID, but many places still keep backup printouts. 

**Current workflows:** Most small restaurants today use one of several ad-hoc methods:  
- **Printed menus or chalkboards:** Traditional solution. It requires printing new copies whenever dishes/prices change, which is time-consuming and wasteful. During rush hours staff must fetch menus or update blackboards manually.  
- **Static digital files:** Some send PDFs (via WhatsApp or email) or post images on social media. This is inflexible (needs re-editing for any change) and hard to keep everywhere updated.  
- **Google Business Profile / Facebook:** Many rely on free listings. Google Business now lets restaurants upload menu items and photos, and Facebook pages often list some info. But these interfaces are cumbersome to maintain (e.g. Google’s menu editor is not rich) and often miss allergen labels.  
- **Specialist apps:** During the pandemic, a surge of “QR menu” apps appeared. E.g. staff create a menu in a web dashboard and print QR codes. Existing services advertise instant updates and analytics. These help customers get menus without waiters, but adoption varies.  

**Workarounds:** Some restaurants simply say “call for menu” on their sign, or list a short menu on Yelp/TripAdvisor without details (a workaround with poor user experience). Others post promotional flyers or “Daily menu” screenshots on Instagram, but those reach followers only and lack interactivity. In sum, there is no universally used tool – each place improvises with paper, PDFs, or general listings.  

# Evidence-backed problems  
We identify several concrete problems (P-01, P-02, …) with evidence and confidence ratings:

- **P-01: Stale or hard-to-update menus.** *Who/situation:* Restaurant staff need to update prices, specials or availability (e.g. “sold out”) but currently do so by reprinting or manually editing PDFs. This is costly and slow. *Consequence:* Menus often go out of date, causing confusion or lost sales. *Evidence:* Competitor sites note that printed menus “can’t be quickly edited” and must be reprinted for every change. QR menu services advertise that their menus are “easy to edit” and save money and time vs. reprinting. *Contra-evidence:* Some restaurants have fixed menus (e.g. standard cafe fare) and may not perceive frequent change as an issue. *Confidence:* Medium. (The literature shows tools emphasize this pain, but we lack surveys of how often small eateries change menus.)

- **P-02: Legal requirement to list allergens.** *Who/situation:* All hospitality businesses in the EU must *always* inform customers about the 14 EU allergens in their dishes (implemented in Spain by RD 126/2015). Staff must ensure these appear “in writing” with the menu content. *Consequence:* Without an easy way to include allergens, restaurants risk non-compliance fines and allergic consumers face safety hazards. *Evidence:* EU Regulation 1169/2011 specifically requires allergen information for non-packaged food like restaurant meals. Menutech and others emphasize that writing allergen names on menus is now mandatory. *Contra-evidence:* No major contra; some small venues still provide verbal allergen info, but this is not compliant. *Confidence:* High. This is codified law.

- **P-03: Language barriers for international guests.** *Who/situation:* Restaurants in tourist areas serve non-Spanish-speaking customers who need translated menu text. *Consequence:* Without translations, restaurants may lose business or cause confusion (and allergy mishaps) with foreign diners. *Evidence:* Multiple menu services highlight automated multi-language support to “serve international visitors”. Menutech offers instant translation into 5 languages for exactly this reason. *Contra-evidence:* Some restaurants rely on staff translation or generic bilingual PDFs; others may prioritize locals over tourists. *Confidence:* Medium. Translation is a known need in hospitality, but specific uptake by small bars is unclear.

- **P-04: Low online visibility and reach.** *Who/situation:* Independent eateries need new customers, especially locals and tourists searching online. Without a proper web presence, they lose out. *Consequence:* Potential diners may skip the venue if they can’t find a menu or photos online, hurting revenue. *Evidence:* QR menu sites claim SEO benefits (e.g. “42% increase in organic reach”) and that digital menus attract foreign guests with language options. Google’s own marketing stresses that sharing menus and dishes on a business profile helps “hungry diners discover what you serve before they walk in”. *Contra-evidence:* Many restaurants get sufficient foot traffic from reputation or location, and may rely on word-of-mouth. Also, Google Business already offers menu features for free. *Confidence:* Low-to-Medium. There’s good reason to believe a digital menu helps, but no hard metrics for small local venues.

- **P-05: Duplication of effort between online and offline menus.** *Who/situation:* If a restaurant maintains both a physical menu and an online menu, staff must update both. *Consequence:* Double work increases errors or makes staff skip online updates entirely. *Evidence:* iMenuPro notes that keeping printed and web menus “in sync” is hard, so they emphasize “Edit once, change everywhere” to avoid multiple versions. Without such integration, staff may neglect the online version. *Contra-evidence:* Some low-budget eateries may skip paper altogether in favor of digital, eliminating the double-work issue entirely. *Confidence:* Medium.

Each problem is well grounded in competitor claims or legal text, but actual prevalence in our target segment is uncertain. Notably, no source reports a survey of how many Spanish restaurants still use only paper menus or ignore digital updates; this is an evidence gap.

# Existing solutions  
Several categories of solutions address (parts of) this module:

- **Dedicated QR Menu services:** These are SaaS platforms where a restaurant staff logs in, enters dishes/prices, and gets a QR code or link. Examples include **QRMenu** (free/paid plans) and **OddMenu**. They offer quick editing, multi-language, analytics, and sometimes ordering. For instance, QRMenu has a free tier that provides a dynamic menu with images and translation. OddMenu’s paid plan ($10/month) offers unlimited items, multilingual menus, “temporary unavailable” toggles, and extras like hiding an item or showing old/new prices.  These require no hardware beyond a smartphone or tablet for editing, and print ready QR stickers. No custom development is needed, but restaurants must pay subscription and rely on the vendor’s service. These solutions vary: QRMenu even offers optional mobile payment (beyond our scope), whereas OddMenu is menu-only. Most emphasize ease of remote editing and engagement (e.g. including images raises “average check”). 

- **Menu-builder & publishing platforms:** Some tools focus on designing menus (for print or web) but also support digital use. **Menutech** (Spain-based) is one: it’s mainly an online design tool, with built-in allergen detection and translation, and can output QR/link versions of menus. Its restaurant plans run from €60–180/month. Another is **iMenuPro**: it started as a desktop/web menu design app, but claims to publish “fast-loading QR menus” (ADA-compliant, no trackers) and sync them to Google, Yelp, TripAdvisor automatically. These platforms integrate menu content with branding/design, but rely on the restaurant to create the content (they don’t fetch dishes from POS, for example). They may also include features beyond our scope (inventory, printing), so we’d use only their menu-share parts.

- **Aggregation/Business profiles:** Most restaurants already appear on platforms like **Google Business Profile**, Facebook, TripAdvisor, etc. These allow basic info and (limited) menu listings. For example, Google now lets restaurants list menu items with photos and prices on their profile. These services are free and widely used, but often lack robust allergen support or easy editing. They also typically require no extra software (business owner simply updates the profile). The trade-off is limited flexibility: you cannot define menu variants or seasonal menus well. Integration with ordering/reservations is available (e.g. Google partners for bookings), which our module omits.

- **Restaurant POS and booking systems:** Although out of scope (because our module excludes orders), many restaurant management systems include some “public menu” feature. For instance, reservation platforms like TheFork or Resy often show a menu on the restaurant’s page, but these are not designed for walk-in ordering. We note that relying on such partial solutions is common: owners list key dishes on reservation sites or apps, but these are not comprehensive menus and not easily editable.

**Pricing and limits:** From above, we see free options exist (Google Business, QRMenu basic). Paid tools range roughly ~$10–20/mo for small restaurants; advanced suites can be €60–180+/mo (Menutech). Paid plans often impose limitations (e.g. QRMenu’s free plan may show vendor branding or limited scans) and require credit cards. Importantly, no major solution is “open source” or one-time cost; most are SaaS. 

**Integrations:** Google/Yelp/TripAdvisor sync is handled by some (e.g. iMenuPro). Translations are offered via proprietary or automatic methods by many tools. None of these directly integrate with a restaurant’s inventory/POS or with Valiris core. Thus any dependency on core data (e.g. hours from establishment) would have to be managed via an API or manual entry.

**Gap identification:** We have not found any solution that is “just a simple site + QR code” bundled with our envisioned core. Everything is either a separate service or part of a bigger ecosystem. We list below what each solution covers vs. our module needs:
- **Menu content management:** All examples cover this (oddmenu says unlimited items). Verified.
- **Multi-language:** Found in QRMenu (90 languages), OddMenu (implied), Menutech (5 languages). Verified.
- **Allergens:** Menutech automates this; other services do not explicitly mention allergen support. Unverified if competitors handle it well or if restaurants still list allergens manually.
- **Pricing/Plans:** Covered in [38], [41], [66]. Verified.
- **Location/Contact Info:** Google Business covers address/hours and can display on menu pages. Competitors (OddMenu etc.) allow adding phone/address. Verified by OddMenu inclusion of address/phone feature (see “Additional restaurant information feature”).
- **No integration with ordering or POS:** Confirmed by product scopes (oddmenu, etc., do not take orders, except QRMenu’s optional payment which is outside our scope).

Where a feature is **not found** explicitly, we mark it unverified. For instance, none of the tools mention VAT compliance or legal disclaimers; however, [18] and [87] imply that the system must *allow* inclusion of allergen info (at least). Lack of advertised functionality does not mean absence; we could support it ourselves. 

# Public experience ledger  
We searched for independent user testimonials or case studies about QR/digital menus in restaurants and found scant direct feedback. No major review site or forum had repeatable narratives. For example, the Menusa and iMenuPro apps have aggregate ratings but no detailed user quotes accessible to cite. Small forums or Reddit threads occasionally debate QR menus (some users remark that QR codes can slow down the dining experience or worry about hygiene), but these are anecdotal and scarce in print. 

**Coverage and limitations:** In lieu of verifiable public reviews, we can only note patterns from available sources. The promotional content of existing products suggests the key perceived *benefits* from user perspective: quicker table turnaround, increased “average check” via upselling, and convenience of contactless menus. Some independent restaurant blogs have reported mixed opinions on QR adoption (citing older diners’ discomfort or app fatigue), but without formal references these are speculative. We therefore do *not* list “R-01, R-02” since our guidelines require citable public accounts. 

In sum, there is an absence of rigorous, public user feedback data specifically on menu modules. This is a gap. The few comments we found (mostly via app store ratings or brief forum posts) tend to align with the advertised pros/cons above. For completeness, any hypothesis or requirement should be validated by direct user research (not available here). 

# Legal and provider constraints  
Relevant regulations and policies for this module include:

- **Food labeling laws:** As noted, EU Regulation 1169/2011 (and Spain’s RD 126/2015) make allergen declaration mandatory for non-packaged foods. Our system must allow restaurants to enter allergens (14 specified types). Nutrition labeling is not required, but allergen and basic ingredients info are. Failure to provide written allergen info is illegal. (No citation needed beyond [18].) 

- **Language and translations:** No legal obligation to translate menus, but marketing/privacy: targeting EU customers may invoke language accessibility under EU services laws (only as guidance, not mandated). There is no EU/Spanish law forcing translations, but consumer protection suggests clarity. Tools often support it, but it is not a compliance issue per se.

- **Accessibility:** In the US, iMenuPro mentions ADA compliance. In Europe, the Web Accessibility Directive (EU 2016/2102) requires public sector sites to meet standards (WCAG), but private restaurant pages are not legally bound. However, including features like adjustable fonts, high contrast, alt text, etc., is good practice. Using “zero trackers” as iMenuPro does can also help with privacy compliance (see below).  No direct law forces us to make a menu page accessible, but it is prudent. We note that if we collect analytics or have any login for staff, GDPR applies.

- **E-Commerce & payments:** Not relevant here since we exclude ordering/payments. If in the future a partner chooses to integrate payments, that would bring PCI/DPA concerns, but out-of-scope for v1.

- **Consumer Data and Privacy:** If the module stores any customer data (e.g. analytics, preferences), GDPR applies. Even without user sign-in, simple analytics (page visits) will involve tracking user IPs. We must follow GDPR for any data retention. Also, the LSSI-CE (Spain’s eCommerce law) requires public websites to display the operator’s identity, contact info and policy links. Since our “storefront” page is public-facing and not behind login, it may trigger LSSI obligations (provide company/contact info). We should provide a privacy policy and cookie notice if we track visits (Mirroring iMenuPro’s “0 trackers” claim suggests user privacy is valued). Using third-party APIs (e.g. Google Maps) will involve agreeing to those terms.

- **External platform policies:** Many restaurants use Google Business or Facebook. Embedding or linking those services means complying with their usage policies (e.g. Google Maps API TOS, Facebook data policies) if we integrate. For QR codes, there are no specific regulations – they just point to a URL. But we should ensure links are HTTPS, etc.

- **Intellectual property:** Menu text and photos belong to the restaurant; our tool is just a medium. We must clarify that the restaurant is responsible for content (to avoid liability issues).  

- **Children’s privacy:** Unlikely to apply (no age target).

In summary, the main legal constraints are food/allergen labeling requirements, EU consumer service standards (fair info, possible translation guidance), and standard privacy laws (GDPR/LSSI). Before launch, we should verify current Spanish guidance (RD numbers above) and ensure our product can meet them (especially allergen labeling). We should draft model privacy and disclaimers that meet LSSI/DPA norms.  

# Opportunities and falsifiable hypotheses  
From the evidence, some needs seem well served and others uncertain. We outline two primary hypotheses:

- **H-01: Restaurants have a real need to replace paper menus with a central digital menu manager.** *Evidence:* Competitor marketing strongly suggests printing is costly and menus become outdated. Our target segment (small, local restaurants) often lacks technical support, so a simple cloud tool could be persuasive. *Assumption:* Staff will use a new tool regularly rather than sticking to word-of-mouth or ad-hoc solutions. *Falsification:* If interviews showed that most owners are satisfied with existing methods (e.g. static PDFs or no change), or that the time to input data into a system outweighs printing effort, then this hypothesis fails. (For example, if an owner needs only a yearly price change, a digital system might be overkill.)

- **H-02: Offering multi-language, updatable menus will attract more customers (especially tourists) and increase sales.** *Evidence:* Services highlight translated menus as a differentiator, and Google’s advice implies menus help diners discover a restaurant. *Assumption:* Tourists or foreign visitors will use the QR page and find it useful enough to dine. *Falsification:* If data shows tourists rely instead on review apps or don’t use QR menus (for instance, older tourists sticking to familiar eateries), this benefit is weaker. We would test by tracking menu page views (if possible) or surveying restaurant owners about foreign patronage.

- **H-03: A standalone menu module (without ordering) is attractive to the target segment.** *Evidence:* Several niche QR menu tools focus only on publishing menus (OddMenu, QRMenu) rather than full commerce, suggesting some demand for non-ordering solutions. *Assumption:* Restaurants want simplicity and may defer adding orders/payments. *Falsification:* If we find that restaurants overwhelmingly prefer a unified system (ordering + menu) – perhaps because they view just-menu apps as a half-measure – then the narrow scope may not capture the market. For example, a restaurant might skip a menu-only tool if they plan to upgrade to an ordering system next year.

- **H-04: Customers will embrace QR and online menus over printed ones, even among older or less tech-savvy groups.** *Evidence:* Many users report the convenience and safety of QR scanning, and it has become common. *Assumption:* By 2026, QR literacy is high. *Falsification:* If surveys show a significant minority of customers actively dislike QR menus or have accessibility issues (e.g. eyesight, device requirements), then focusing solely on digital menus might alienate them. (For instance, if an elderly demographic frequently requests a paper menu or manual service, the digital solution might not satisfy all guests.)

We note these hypotheses are **testable**: through interviews with restaurant owners and customers, we can measure current methods (for H-01), tourist usage (H-02), feature preferences (H-03), and customer satisfaction (H-04).  

# Implications for M0.3  
The research raises several questions for the product definition phase:  

- **Integration with existing tools:** Should we integrate directly with Google Business or social media for listing menus, or focus on our own page? If many restaurants already use Google profiles, we might allow syncing (GDPR and API permitting) rather than duplicate effort. We need to clarify dependencies on mapping and review platforms (e.g. embedding Google Maps for location requires using Google’s API).  

- **Scope of management console:** What exactly will staff enter? Since our core handles establishment data, perhaps location/hours come from there, leaving only menu items to input. We must specify if menu items and allergen tags are the only content. Also, who in the restaurant can edit — do we use core membership roles, or a separate admin interface?  

- **Allergen and translation features:** Evidence strongly suggests we need built-in allergen labeling (for compliance). The method (automatic vs manual) must be decided: Menutech’s auto-detection hints we could use a database or AI to suggest allergens. We should verify accuracy. For translation, we must choose whether to implement ourselves (via an API) or rely on the user to input translations. The “instant translation” claim implies high-language support, but machine-translation quality and cost need review.  

- **Mobile friendliness:** Given the “contactless” and on-the-go use cases, the page must be highly optimized for phones. Tests for load speed, readability (no pinch-zoom needed), and offline fallback should be planned. The iMenuPro emphasis on “fast-loading, no trackers” underscores this. 

- **Fallback and print:** Even if digital is main, we might still support printing a static menu from the data (for restaurants that keep a paper copy). This was an implicit need (“print or share”). We must decide if that’s in-scope or recommended integration.

- **Business model considerations:** Though not required now, evidence of free vs paid solutions should inform pricing. If many alternatives exist, our tool might need a freemium approach or justify its value (e.g. integration with other core modules). We may hypothesize a free tier or one-time fee to encourage trial.

- **Technical stack decisions:** We should confirm any external dependencies: e.g. translation API (Google Translate?), map embedding (Google Maps or OpenStreetMap?), QR generation (likely trivial). Content delivery: do we host on Valiris domain or a separate domain?

- **User research gaps:** The lack of clear user feedback means M0.3 should include primary user interviews or surveys. We need to validate usage frequency of digital menus, actual pain points with current methods, and willingness to adopt new systems.  

These questions must be resolved before writing detailed requirements. We must avoid assuming all features (e.g. translation, analytics) are needed without confirming demand.
