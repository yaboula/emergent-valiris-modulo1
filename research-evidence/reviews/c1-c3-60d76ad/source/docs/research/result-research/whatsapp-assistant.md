# Executive Conclusion
The WhatsApp Assistant module addresses persistent communication gaps in local restaurants and cafés. Customers overwhelmingly prefer instant messaging (75% of adults want to message businesses), and WhatsApp has >2 billion users. Yet many establishments rely on phone calls or in-person queries, leading to unanswered questions about opening hours, location, menu items, allergies, or booking conditions – especially outside business hours. A WhatsApp-based FAQ bot could instantly serve these common queries and initiate reservations. For example, ChatBot.com notes that a restaurant chatbot can provide *“quick access to opening hours, menu information, location details, and reservation policies,”* and even collect booking requests. If well integrated, this could reduce front-desk workload and increase booking conversions.

However, key uncertainties remain. We lack evidence on actual request volumes via WhatsApp or on booking outcomes. The module must respect strict WhatsApp policies (explicit opt-in, use of approved templates, content rules) and handle sensitive info (e.g. allergens) safely. It also depends on data from the core (menus, availability). Before committing to requirements, we must test whether typical diners will use a WhatsApp bot to book tables or seek info, and whether owners will trust its answers. We should also verify that current alternatives (phone, basic WhatsApp Business app auto-replies) *cannot* already handle these tasks adequately. The strongest evidence is the broad preference for messaging communication and vendor reports of large volumes handled by chatbots (e.g. 125k chats/year with 18% workload reduction). The main counterpoint is that some restaurants may not have high enough chat volume to justify automation, and legal/privacy rules (GDPR, e-commerce laws) impose restraints.

# Module Boundary
This investigation focuses **only** on a WhatsApp-based assistant that answers diner inquiries (hours, location, menu, reservation rules) and helps initiate table bookings. It *excludes* features explicitly ruled out (takeaway/delivery orders, payment, invoicing, POS integration, loyalty or marketing beyond booking, and detailed menu ordering). We assume core features (restaurant profile, menu data, booking system) exist, but the assistant’s internal logic (NLP/AI) is not yet defined. We also limit to direct customer queries via WhatsApp. Anything outside WhatsApp (e.g. phone calls, web chat, email) is out of scope.

# Users, Situations and Current Workflows
**Primary users:** restaurant owners/managers or staff, and their customers (diners). In practice, staff (host/manager) currently field queries by phone or in person. Customers often ask about *opening hours, address, menu details (dish ingredients or daily specials), reservation options, or holiday closures*. For sit-down restaurants they may request a table; for walk-in cafés they might ask if they accept bookings or how long waits are. According to WhatsApp (2020), 75% of adults want to connect with businesses via messaging, so many customers would expect to simply text a restaurant.

**Situations:** A diner might text late in the evening to confirm the next day’s hours, or send a photo of an allergen card asking if a dish is gluten-free, or inquire “can we book a table for 6?” on a busy weekend. Today, staff typically answer by phone during open hours. If the phone isn’t answered, messages may be seen hours later (if at all). Some businesses use the free WhatsApp Business app to display hours and send generic “away” messages, but these are static. Others have no online presence beyond maybe a Facebook page. If customers cannot get timely info, they may go elsewhere or reserve on a third-party site (e.g. TheFork, Google).

**Alternatives and workflows:** Common current workflows include: maintaining printed or website menus, answering phone calls, and relying on Google Maps or Facebook for basic info. Some use spreadsheets or diary notes for bookings. Few small restaurants have a dedicated booking system or automated chat. In Spain, it is common for customers to text a known restaurant number (often found via Google) for quick queries or to try to book last-minute. A 24/7 WhatsApp assistant could handle off-hours messages and recurrent FAQs without tying up staff.

# Evidence-Backed Problems
- **P-01: Repetitive FAQs consume staff time.** (*User:* restaurant staff; *situation:* daily high volume of simple queries; *consequence:* inefficient use of time, possible missed info.)  Many questions are routine (hours, location, menu, booking policy). ChatBot.com notes a restaurant chatbot “can give guests quick access to opening hours, menu information, location details, and reservation policies”. Without automation, staff spend minutes per query; over time this adds up. A supporting data point: ChatBot.com cites one client automating 125k chats/year, cutting workload 18%. *Counterevidence:* The free WhatsApp Business app offers simple auto-replies/labels, partially reducing effort, but cannot dynamically answer questions outside preset messages. Staff may not track how many messages they currently miss or answer, but industry study suggests two-thirds of consumers prefer chat, implying unserved demand. *Confidence:* Medium – logical and supported by vendor case-study, but lacking independent metrics for small restaurants.

- **P-02: Customers get delayed or no responses.** (*User:* diners; *situation:* asking outside office hours or during peak times; *consequence:* frustration, lost bookings.) If a customer texts about availability after-hours, there is no immediate answer. If calls are busy, they may try multiple times or give up. The WhatsApp blog emphasizes meeting customers in their preferred channel, noting high global usage. *Counterevidence:* Some tech-savvy customers might use Google Maps for basic info (hours are often listed there) or decide based on online menus. Also, not all diners use WhatsApp to book; older customers might still phone. *Confidence:* Medium – the broad statistic suggests demand, but restaurant-specific frequency is uncertain.

- **P-03: Reservation process lacks automation.** (*User:* diners/restaurant; *situation:* booking a table via WhatsApp; *consequence:* no integration, manual work.) If a diner asks “table for 4 at 8pm?”, staff must manually note it, risking double-booking or errors. The assistant could gather booking details and pass them on. ChatBot.com says a chatbot “can collect a reservation request or direct guests to the restaurant’s booking page”, but confirming needs a real reservation system. *Counterevidence:* Some businesses already use apps (TheFork, Resy) which handle reservations and sync calendars; these often display their booking link prominently, reducing manual work. If the assistant simply says “we have openings” without system check, it risks error. *Confidence:* Low – potential benefit is clear, but need to verify if local restaurants in target segment already manage small volumes manually fine.

- **P-04: Sensitive queries (allergies, dietary) require caution.** (*User:* diners; *situation:* asking about ingredients/allergens; *consequence:* risk of misinformation.) Customers frequently ask “Is your paella vegetarian?” or “Which dishes contain nuts?” ChatBot.com warns that allergy questions should come from well-maintained data and escalate to staff as needed. Currently, staff answer from memory or refer to printed allergen lists (per EU law, allergens *must* be declared). An automated bot might inadvertently give incorrect advice. Misleading safety/health info could have liability implications. *Counterevidence:* If the assistant is strictly based on the restaurant’s own menu metadata, risk is low. However, full compliance (e.g. Reg.1169/2011 EU) demands caution. *Confidence:* High – clearly identified risk, as noted by ChatBot.com.

# Existing Solutions
We identify several approaches:

- **WhatsApp Business App (Meta)**: A free mobile app for SMEs. It lets restaurants set business hours, store info, use fixed *Quick Replies* and automated greeting/away messages. **Capabilities:** limited rule-based responses (e.g. user sends “menu”, it can reply with a saved text or catalog link). **Limits:** No natural language understanding or reservation workflow. Only one-person handling (no multi-agent inbox). **Integration:** Not integrable with external systems (each staffer must view messages on a phone). **Pricing:** Free with SIM number. **Suitability:** Good for very small shops; insufficient for true “assistant” behavior.

- **Meta’s WhatsApp Business Platform (API)**: Enables chatbots and multi-user inboxes. Requires a provider (e.g. Twilio, Vonage) and business verification. **Capabilities:** Automated replies to user-initiated chats (24h window free), and pre-approved *template messages* for outbound notifications (e.g. booking confirmations). Supports text, images, buttons. **Limits:** Must obtain user opt-in. Business-initiated messages outside 24h need templates (no free-form). **Pricing:** Meta charges per message (country-dependent) plus provider fees; often a small per-message fee (e.g. ~$0.01–$0.05). Usually not cost-effective for very low volumes. **Integration:** High; connects to CRM, booking system, AI. **Example Vendors:** Twilio, MessageBird, Smooch.

- **Chatbot Builders (Landbot, ManyChat, Chatfuel, ChatBot.com, etc.)**: No-code platforms to design conversational flows. Many now support WhatsApp (Landbot has native WhatsApp bots). **Capabilities:** Can script multi-step conversations (gathering name/date, etc.), integrate with booking calendars or databases, and use AI/NLP. **Limits:** Monthly subscription (often starting ~$30–$50, scaling by number of chats or users). Some handle fallback to human chat. They generally *cannot* themselves push WhatsApp messages; they use the Business API via a provider. **Integration:** Often integrate with CRM/Google Sheets/booking APIs via Zapier or webhooks. **Popularity:** Landbot advertises WhatsApp sales support and booking bots; ChatBot.com (LiveChat) markets restaurant-specific AI bots. **Reviews:** Vendors claim high satisfaction (ChatBot.com shows 4.6/5 Capterra). Independent reviews note steep learning curve and need to maintain content.

- **Unified Inbox Tools (Crisp, Zendesk, HubSpot, etc.)**: Customer service suites with WhatsApp channels. For example, Crisp offers a shared team inbox where WhatsApp chats appear alongside email/Facebook messages. **Capabilities:** Human agents manage chats with some canned replies, and may add basic chatbot automations. **Limits:** Focused on live support, not AI Q&A. Automation is limited to simple FAQs unless custom-built. **Pricing:** Typically subscriptions per team seat (from ~$20/user/month). Some have a free tier for small volumes.

- **Booking Platforms (TheFork, OpenTable, Google Reserve, Wix Bookings)**: These handle reservations via web or app. **Capabilities:** Provide booking widget/links and some messaging (e.g. email confirmations, SMS reminders). **Limits:** Not integrated with WhatsApp; the customer books through those apps/sites, not by chatting with the restaurant. They don’t answer spontaneous WhatsApp queries at all. **Pricing:** TheFork charges per cover booked or a fixed fee; Google Reserve may take a commission.

No solution found fully covers both 24/7 messaging Q&A and booking in one. Chatbot platforms can *implement* such flows, but require custom setup and cost. Most small restaurants currently stick to phones or simple WhatsApp Business App (which is absent from competitor listings because it’s generic).

# Legal and Provider Constraints
- **L-01 (WhatsApp Opt-In Requirement):** Businesses must obtain explicit user consent before messaging them. For restaurants, this means the customer must initiate contact or otherwise agree (e.g. by clicking a WhatsApp link after ordering). Unsolicited messages (e.g. promotional blasts) are prohibited. *Implication:* The assistant can *only respond* to inbound user messages (user-initiated or via approved templates within policy); it cannot start a conversation except via approved template (e.g. confirmation messages).

- **L-02 (Template and 24h Rules):** Meta enforces a 24-hour “free messaging” window from the last user message. Outside that, any business-initiated message must use an approved template (with restricted format). Templates must fit allowed categories (marketing, utility, authentication, service) and can only contain certain content (no promotional content in service messages, etc.). *Implication:* The assistant can send free-form answers only within the 24h of user’s latest query. For follow-ups after that, we must use generic templates (e.g. “Your reservation is confirmed”). This limits push notifications or reminders (they require templates, and the user must have opted in to notifications).

- **L-03 (Data Privacy & Consent):** Under EU GDPR (and Spain’s LOPDGDD), personal data (like phone number, names) must be collected lawfully. The opt-in to WhatsApp itself can serve as consent to process the conversation data. However, any storage of conversation logs must be covered by a privacy policy and kept to the minimum required. If the assistant uses personal data (e.g. name, contact info), the restaurant must allow users to delete or refuse data use. *Implication:* Even though customers voluntarily message, the business must handle their data responsibly (e.g. giving an unsubscribe or STOP keyword on WhatsApp if collecting mobile numbers).

- **L-04 (Electronic Commerce Law – Spain):** Spain’s law 34/2002 on e-commerce forbids sending commercial electronic communications without prior consent. WhatsApp messages are considered electronic communications, so any promotional content via the assistant (e.g. announcing special offers) requires explicit opt-in. Non-promotional service messages (booking confirmations, reservation reminders) are allowed if the user has engaged. *Implication:* The assistant should avoid sending any marketing or menus unless requested.

- **L-05 (Food/Health Regulations):** EU Regulation 1169/2011 and Spanish food safety laws mandate accurate allergen and nutritional information. A chatbot must rely on officially provided data; misinforming a customer about allergens could violate law and cause liability. *Implication:* The assistant should flag sensitive queries (allergens, medical advice) and defer to human staff or disclaim information, rather than guessing. This is also a design guideline to comply with Meta’s “health and wellness” policy (discussing food can edge into health advice if not careful).

- **L-06 (WhatsApp Terms of Service):** The WhatsApp Terms and Commerce Policies forbid certain content (e.g. “politics, adult, hate speech, illegal goods”). For our module, relevant rules are around prohibited content and trademarks. For example, the assistant cannot send copyrighted menu photos without permission. Also, per Meta’s Commerce Policy, promotions (e.g. lotteries or gambling) are limited. *Implication:* Stick to factual, benign content.

# Opportunities and Falsifiable Hypotheses

- **H-01: Automating FAQs saves staff time.** *Evidence:* ChatBot.com reports an 18% support workload reduction when automating chat inquiries. We hypothesize that a WhatsApp assistant handling routine queries (hours, menu, bookings) will similarly free staff for other tasks. *Assumption:* Query volume is significant. *Test:* Pilot in a few restaurants measuring number of questions answered by bot vs staff time. *Counter:* If customer queries are rare, benefits will be small.

- **H-02: Diners will use the assistant.** *Evidence:* 75% of adults prefer messaging to contact businesses and WhatsApp is ubiquitous. Hypothesis: a significant portion (>50%) of tech-savvy diners will text the restaurant via WhatsApp if available. *Assumption:* Diners notice the WhatsApp channel (via signage or online listing). *Test:* Offer WhatsApp contact prominently and track chat initiations. *Counter:* Some diners may still call or walk in; also, elderly patrons might not use WhatsApp.

- **H-03: A WhatsApp booking feature increases reservations.** *Assumption:* Convenient booking via chat encourages more reservations (especially last-minute). *Evidence:* ChatBot.com notes chatbots *“can collect a reservation request”*. *Test:* Compare booking rates before/after assistant launch. *Counter:* If the restaurant already fills via other channels, incremental gain may be small.

- **H-04: The assistant will improve customer satisfaction.** *Assumption:* Instant answers reduce frustration. *Evidence:* ChatBots aim for 24/7 support; 93% satisfaction reported in one case. *Test:* Survey customers pre- and post-deployment. *Counter:* Poor bot performance (misunderstandings) could annoy customers, or some might find automation impersonal.

- **H-05: Integration costs are manageable.** *Assumption:* The restaurant’s core system can expose necessary info (hours, menus, availability). If core data is incomplete or requires manual upkeep, the project may fail. *Test:* Inventory core data readiness; prototype the integration. *Counter:* If integration is too complex or expensive, the assistant remains theoretical.

# Implications for M0.3
Key questions and potential requirements emerging from above:
- *Data integration:* How to fetch current hours, menu, and availability from the core? The assistant will need up-to-date content. (E.g. link to the catalogue or store menu in a database.)
- *Fallback to humans:* The assistant should hand off to staff for complex questions (allergies, booking exceptions). We might require a “transfer” feature or at least a notification to human staff.
- *Templates:* We must prepare approved message templates (e.g. “Your reservation for [time] is confirmed”) ahead of launch. Early analysis: identify which notifications need templates.
- *Opt-in handling:* Define how users give consent. Possibly by clicking a WhatsApp link on the website or scanning a QR with a privacy notice. The system must record opt-ins to comply.
- *Logging:* Given GDPR, chat transcripts might need to be archived securely. Should we allow users to delete their data?
- *Scope of answers:* Decide exact FAQ scope (hours, directions, menu items, reservation policies). Ensure they are maintainable (e.g. use core data or CMS).
- *Booking workflow:* If we allow bookings, do we integrate with the “Table Bookings” core? If not, perhaps just send requests to staff. This needs clarification.
- *Language support:* The module likely should handle Spanish primarily (and possibly Catalan or English depending on location). We should consider multi-language capabilities.
- *WhatsApp policies:* We need to define what content is automated vs manual. For example, after 24h without user message, no automated check-in or marketing push can be done unless user re-initiates or a template applies (see [61] and [65]).

None of these are final decisions; they are development questions for M0.3. They stem from evidence (policy citations and identified needs) and must be validated (e.g. by a legal review of templates, or by prototypes).
